# Learning and memory

Use this reference for lessons, practice, and study plans. These are adaptable teaching choices, not a mandatory layout for every answer. They support comprehension and recall without claiming to measure the reader's brain.

## Choose what the learner should be able to do

Focus on the requested outcome: explain an idea, distinguish two ideas, solve a problem, or perform a task. Use the learner's stated knowledge and attempts to identify prerequisites. In an ordinary explanation, infer a reasonable starting point and proceed. In interactive tutoring, one short diagnostic problem can reveal a gap more usefully than a broad question about intelligence or learning style.

Choose a representation that fits the subject:

| Learning need | Useful form |
|---|---|
| Understand a mechanism | Concrete case, relevant parts, causal relationships, then what changes under a different condition |
| Learn a procedure | Worked example with reasons, a similar attempt, then less guidance as performance improves |
| Understand an abstract relationship | A concrete instance mapped explicitly to the general rule; another instance to test transfer |
| Distinguish ideas or interpretations | A shared comparison criterion, supporting evidence, and the condition under which each applies |

When explaining history, social systems, or disputed claims, retain uncertainty and alternative explanations. A neat story must not manufacture a single cause. When teaching mathematics or technical material, introduce the plain-language meaning and the formal term or notation together.

## Support attention and understanding

Show why the concept matters to the user's question. Keep each part focused on one meaningful relationship. Make the link to the next part explicit. A concrete example should expose the important relationship; decorative stories, extra facts, and clever analogies can distract from it.

For unfamiliar procedures, start with enough guidance for the learner to follow the reasoning. Reduce help as they demonstrate competence. Explanation and practice have different jobs: a clear worked example introduces the method, while the learner's attempt reveals whether they can use it.

## Recall, application, and feedback

After instruction, an optional check can ask the learner to explain the central idea from memory, explain why a step works, or apply it to a slightly different case. One or two prompts are often enough for a chat response; this is a design choice, not a memory limit.

In a standalone lesson, place the answer key after the prompts with clear separation and an invitation to attempt them first. In an explicitly interactive lesson, wait for the learner's answer. Do not withhold an explanation the user requested as a condition of passing a quiz.

Feedback should identify what is correct, explain the specific error, and show how to repair it. If a learner cannot start, provide a cue or another worked example. If they succeed, use a new case or reduce guidance. For skill acquisition, include actual problem solving or performance; memorizing a definition alone does not establish competence.

## Revisit over time

For a requested study plan, propose recall on a later occasion before looking at the explanation again. An initial plan might revisit a concept the next day and the following week. Label those intervals as an adjustable starting point, not a scientifically optimal schedule. Consider the eventual use date and the learner's success: review sooner after difficulty, and use longer gaps after reliable recall.

Reading fluency and confidence are insufficient measures of learning. Evidence of understanding includes an accurate explanation or successful application to a new case. Evidence of retention requires checking again after a delay.

## Evidence and limits

The papers below support component techniques. The choices of prompt count, response length, and chat layout are practical design decisions. Effects depend on the material, prior knowledge, practice conditions, and what the assessment measures.

| Source | What it supports and its practical limit |
|---|---|
| [W3C: Clear and understandable content](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/objectives/o3-clear-content/) | Accessibility guidance favors familiar words, clear sentences, and manageable text blocks. This guidance does not establish a universal retention benefit or a fixed word limit. |
| [Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/) | In experiments with prose, retrieval produced better delayed recall than restudy, while restudy could look better immediately. This does not mean every difficult problem should replace initial teaching. |
| [Butler & Roediger, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18491500/) | Feedback after multiple-choice practice improved later correct recall and reduced recall of misleading options. Correct errors rather than leaving them unexamined. |
| [Cepeda et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/) | In fact-learning experiments, the most effective study gap depended on the later test delay. There is no single best spacing schedule for every learning goal. |
| [Chi et al., 1994](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3) | A small study of a circulatory-system text found improved understanding when learners explained the material to themselves. Use focused explanation prompts without assuming the same gain in all settings. |
| [Kalyuga, Chandler & Sweller, 2001](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01443410124681) | Inexperienced apprentices benefited from worked examples on complex tasks, with benefits changing as experience grew. Adapt guidance to demonstrated knowledge. |
| [IES: Organizing instruction and study](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1) | This practice guide supports spaced study, examples combined with practice, integrated graphics and words, and links between concrete and abstract representations. It synthesizes evidence with differing strength across recommendations. |
| [Pashler et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/) | The review found insufficient evidence for matching instruction to diagnosed learning-style categories. Respect preferences and accessibility needs without claiming a preferred format is a biological learning type. |

Avoid neuroscience claims about dopamine, brain wiring, retention percentages, or fixed attention spans unless a relevant source supports the specific claim. A simpler explanation can reduce avoidable effort; some useful learning still requires effortful recall and practice.
