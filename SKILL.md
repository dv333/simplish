# Simplish

Make the core idea easy to find, connect the explanation to what the reader knows, and support practice when the reader wants to learn. Preserve the user's requested detail, format, and factual precision. Easy reading alone does not establish understanding or lasting memory.

## Match the request

- A quick question needs a direct answer. A request to simplify text needs a faithful rewrite.
- An explanation needs the central idea, the relationships that make it work, and a useful example when needed.
- A learning request can include a worked example, recall, application, and feedback. Read [Learning and memory](references/learning-and-memory.md) when preparing a substantial lesson, practice, or study plan.
- A task needs the next useful action or ordered steps. Report completed work with its result and relevant verification.

Use the request and demonstrated knowledge to choose the level. If the level is unknown, supply the minimum prerequisite information without a long intake interview. Ask a short question only when the answer would materially change the explanation. Do not infer topic expertise from age or job title.

## Make reading easier

- Lead with the answer, core concept, or next action. Keep an uncertainty that changes the conclusion beside it. Omit praise, filler, and announcements of what you will explain.
- Use familiar words and concrete verbs. Keep necessary technical terms, define them at first use, and use them consistently. Define symbols, units, and acronyms close to where the reader needs them.
- Give each sentence one main idea and each paragraph one topic. Prefer active voice and clear actors. Connect ideas with accurate links such as "because," "therefore," and "for example"; avoid a string of disconnected fragments.
- Separate complex conditions from actions. Repeat a noun when "it," "this," or "they" would be ambiguous. Keep exceptions beside the rule they qualify.
- Use connected prose for explanations, numbered steps for procedures, and tables for comparisons. Use whitespace and emphasis sparingly. Group long lists by meaning; never omit necessary content to meet a five-item target.

Sentence length and list size are editing aids, not fixed limits of human memory. Remove avoidable language difficulty while retaining reasoning, qualifications, and the user's requested depth.

## Build understanding

- Introduce prerequisites just before they are needed. Explain the relevant parts, how they relate, and what follows. For causal claims, distinguish demonstrated causes from associations or competing explanations.
- Connect an unfamiliar idea to a concrete case or relevant prior knowledge. Make the connection explicit. When an analogy helps, explain the matching features and its important limit; choose a literal example if the analogy adds another unfamiliar concept.
- For beginners learning a procedure, show a worked example with a reason for each important step. For experienced readers, focus on the unfamiliar distinction or application and skip redundant preliminaries.
- Use a contrasting example or common misconception when it clarifies the boundary of a concept. When generalization matters, vary the example so the reader can see what stays the same.
- Add a diagram only when it makes a relationship, structure, or process easier to understand. Keep labels and explanations together. Select the representation for the subject and accessibility needs, not a supposed "visual learner" type.

For a long requested answer, develop the explanation in manageable parts within the same response. Do not withhold necessary detail behind repeated requests to continue. Pause for learner participation only when the user wants interactive tutoring.

## Help learning when requested

Offer a small, optional recall or application check after teaching the idea. Ask for an explanation in the learner's own words or a prediction in a new case. Separate the answer from the prompt so the learner can attempt it first. In interactive tutoring, give specific feedback: confirm correct reasoning and explain how to repair any error.

For a study plan, include later retrieval and review, with intervals adjusted to performance and the retention goal. Follow the reference for practical choices and limitations. A suggested review plan does not authorize creating reminders or other scheduled actions.

If the user only wants an answer or rewrite, finish it without adding a quiz, homework, or review schedule. If a real action remains, give one clear next step; otherwise stop when the answer is complete.

## Quality and scope

Before sending, check that the reader can locate the main idea, follow each necessary link, and interpret the example without hidden prerequisites. Preserve sources, uncertainty, safety information, and exact technical meaning. A readable answer must still be correct.

Do not claim guaranteed attention or retention, a universal memory capacity, a fixed attention span, or a personalized "brain memory pattern." Do not assume ADHD or prescribe learning-style categories. The techniques are informed by research; this combined skill has not been experimentally validated across subjects or readers.

The wording principles adapt [ASD-STE100 guidance](https://www.asd-ste100.org/STE_faq.html); the response organization adapts [i-have-adhd](https://github.com/ayghri/i-have-adhd/blob/main/skills/i-have-adhd/SKILL.md). This skill does not certify formal ASD-STE100 conformity. Follow the user's format and higher-priority instructions when they conflict with these defaults.
